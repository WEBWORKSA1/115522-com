Lucky gift amounts around the world

Updated 8 October 20266 min readBy the 115522 editorial team

The "right" amount of gift money depends on where you are. In India, amounts end in 1 (₹101, ₹501, ₹1,100); in China, even amounts with 8 are lucky and 4 is avoided; in Japan and Korea, odd amounts are preferred for many occasions; and in Vietnam, crisp new notes in a red envelope matter as much as the sum. This guide explains the reasoning behind each custom and ends with a quick reference table.

India: shagun amounts ending in 1

At weddings, birthdays, housewarmings and religious ceremonies, Indian families commonly give cash as shagun in amounts such as ₹101, ₹251, ₹501, ₹1,001, ₹1,100 or ₹2,100. Many gift envelopes sold in India even come with a one-rupee coin attached.

Why the extra one?

Several explanations are given, and families often cite more than one:

  • Avoiding a zero ending. A round number such as 500 ends in zero, which some associate with an ending. Adding 1 turns it into a beginning.
  • Indivisibility. An amount like 501 cannot be split evenly into two, which is read as a wish that the couple's fortunes stay undivided.
  • Continuity. The extra rupee symbolises a relationship that continues, a light "debt" that will be returned at the giver's own celebration.
  • Growth. The additional coin is a seed meant to grow, and a metal coin is also associated with Lakshmi, the goddess of wealth.

Practical notes: new or clean notes are preferred, the money is usually placed in a decorated envelope, and the amount typically rises with closeness of the relationship. For regional Indian traditions around specific numbers, such as 786 or 108, see our lucky numbers guide.

China: red envelopes, even amounts and 8

Red envelopes, called hongbao in Mandarin and lai see in Cantonese, are given at Lunar New Year, weddings, birthdays and to employees as bonuses. The main rules:

  • Even amounts for happy occasions. Good things are said to come in pairs, while odd amounts are associated with funerals.
  • 8 is the luckiest digit because it sounds like the word for prosperity. Amounts that contain or end in 8 are popular.
  • 6 and 9 are also good: 6 suggests smooth progress, and 9 sounds like "long-lasting", which suits weddings.
  • Avoid 4, which sounds like the word for death. An amount like 400 or 44 would be a serious misstep.
  • Use crisp new notes and avoid coins. Give and receive with both hands.

Love numbers: 520 and 1314

Some amounts are read as words. 520 sounds like "I love you", and 1314 suggests "for a lifetime". Couples send 520 or 1314 yuan as digital red packets, and 20 May (5/20) has become an informal internet Valentine's Day in China. WeChat normally caps a single red packet at 200 yuan, but on 20 May 2016 it raised the cap to 520 yuan for the day so that users could send the full amount.

Japan: odd amounts and new bills

Japanese cash gifts follow some of the most precise etiquette anywhere.

  • Weddings (goshugi). About ¥30,000 is the standard for a friend or colleague, with ¥50,000 or more for a superior or close relative. The amount should start with an odd digit, because an even number can be divided, which is unwelcome symbolism for a marriage. To give ¥20,000, some people use one ¥10,000 note and two ¥5,000 notes so the number of bills is odd.
  • Avoid 4 and 9. 4 can be read shi, like death, and 9 can be read ku, like suffering.
  • New bills for celebrations. Wedding money should be in crisp, unused notes, showing you prepared carefully.
  • Used bills for funerals (koden). New notes would imply you expected the death. If you only have new ones, people crease them first. Typical funeral gifts are smaller, about ¥5,000 for a friend or colleague.
  • Envelopes matter. Celebration envelopes use a bow-style mizuhiki cord that can be retied, because the occasion is welcome again; funeral envelopes use a tight knot that cannot.

South Korea: odd numbers and rising norms

Korean cash gifts follow similar logic. For condolence money at funerals, amounts beginning with an odd digit, such as ₩30,000, ₩50,000 or ₩70,000, are considered appropriate. ₩100,000 is also accepted, partly because 10 can be seen as the sum of two odd numbers, 3 and 7.

For weddings, the long-standing default of ₩50,000 is giving way to ₩100,000. An NH NongHyup Bank analysis reported in May 2026 found that the share of ₩100,000 wedding gifts rose from 36.1% in 2023 to 39.7% in 2025, while ₩50,000 gifts fell from 46.5% to 42.3%. The average wedding gift in 2025 was about ₩117,000. As in Japan and China, 4 is generally avoided across Korean culture because it sounds like the Sino-Korean word for death.

Vietnam: li xi at Tet

During Tet, the Lunar New Year, Vietnamese families give li xi (lucky money) in red envelopes.

  • Who gives: mostly adults to children, but adult children also give to elderly parents as thanks, and employers to staff.
  • New notes: fresh, uncreased banknotes are strongly preferred, and many people visit banks or exchange services before Tet to get them.
  • Always in an envelope: handing over loose cash is considered improper.
  • Avoid 4, which sounds like death, and lean towards even amounts.
  • Typical sums: about 20,000 to 100,000 dong for children, 50,000 to 200,000 for extended family and close friends, and 200,000 dong to a million or more for elderly relatives.

Western countries: fewer number rules

In the US, Canada, the UK and much of Europe, the digits in a gift amount carry little meaning. Round figures are normal, and there is no expectation of new notes.

  • Weddings. Cash or a registry gift is acceptable. Etiquette writers say it is a myth that you must "cover your plate"; give what suits your budget and relationship.
  • Tipping. In the US, 15% to 20% of the pre-tax restaurant bill is the usual standard. Tipping norms vary a lot by country, and service charges are often included in Europe.
  • Superstition. Western number superstition centres on 13, but it rarely affects gift amounts.

Tips when you are a guest in another culture

  1. Ask a local friend about the usual range; amounts vary by city and relationship.
  2. Get new notes from a bank a few days early, especially before Lunar New Year.
  3. Check that your total does not accidentally contain a 4.
  4. Use the right envelope: red for Chinese and Vietnamese celebrations, and the correct style for Japanese weddings and funerals.
  5. When unsure, follow the local rule for odd or even amounts and err on the generous side.

Questions

Why do Indians add one rupee to gift money?

The extra rupee avoids an amount ending in zero, makes the sum indivisible, and symbolises continuity and growth. It is common at weddings and other celebrations.

How much should I put in a Chinese red envelope?

Use an even amount in crisp new notes, ideally containing 8, 6 or 9, and avoid any 4. The exact sum depends on your relationship and local norms.

How much do you give at a Japanese wedding?

About ¥30,000 is standard for a friend or colleague, in new notes and a proper envelope. Amounts should start with an odd digit, and 4 and 9 are avoided.

What do 520 and 1314 mean?

In Chinese, 520 sounds like 'I love you' and 1314 suggests 'for a lifetime'. Couples use them as gift amounts, especially on 20 May.
